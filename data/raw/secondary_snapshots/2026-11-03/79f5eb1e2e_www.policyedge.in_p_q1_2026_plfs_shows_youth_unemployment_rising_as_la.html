Q1 2026 PLFS Shows Youth Unemployment Rising as Labour Participation Softens

Overall unemployment remained unchanged from a year earlier, but weaker participation and a rise in joblessness among young women point to a softer labour market in April-June 2026

SDG 5: Gender Equality | SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth

Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation MoSPI | National Statistical Office NSO

Key Details

The Periodic Labour Force Survey Quarterly Bulletin for April-June 2026 provides national labour-market estimates covering both rural and urban areas.

  • Overall unemployment: 5.4%, up from 5% in the preceding quarter but unchanged from April-June 2025

  • Youth unemployment: 15.9%, compared with 15% in January-March 2026 and 14.6% a year earlier

  • Labour-force participation: 54.6%, down from 55.5% in the preceding quarter and 55% a year earlier

  • Worker-population ratio: 51.7%, down from 52.8% in the preceding quarter and 52% a year earlier

  • Rural unemployment: 4.8%, compared with 6.7% in urban areas

  • Estimated workers: Approximately 56.57 crore people aged 15 and above were employed during the quarter

Headline Unemployment Masks Softer Participation

The unemployment rate for people aged 15 and above remained at 5.4%, unchanged from a year earlier. But other indicators point to some weakening in labour-market participation.

Compared with the preceding quarter, the Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) fell by 0.9 percentage points, while the Worker Population Ratio (WPR) declined by 1.1 percentage points.

This distinction matters. Unemployment measures joblessness only among people working or actively seeking and available for work. If people leave or do not enter the labour force, unemployment can remain stable even as the share of the population actually working declines.

Youth Employment Shows the Clearest Weakness

Unemployment among people aged 15–29 years rose to 15.9%, its highest level across the five quarters reported under the revamped survey series.

-Advertisement-

The deterioration was particularly pronounced among young women:

  • Female youth unemployment: 16% → 19.6%

  • Rural female youth unemployment: 12.4% → 17.1%

  • Urban female youth unemployment: remained high at 25.2%

  • Male youth unemployment: 14.1% → 14.5%

The youth WPR also declined from 35.8% to 34.3% over the year, meaning a smaller share of young people was working during the reference week.

Education and training keep some young people outside the labour force, so these figures do not represent the proportion of all youth without jobs. Even so, the combination of rising unemployment and falling employmentpoints to greater difficulty among young people seeking to enter or remain in work.

Women’s Participation Remains Low and Uneven

Among women aged 15 and above, LFPR stood at 33.2%, compared with 77% for men. The rural-urban divide remained substantial: female participation was 37.2% in rural areas and 25% in urban areas, while corresponding employment rates were 35.4% and 22.8%.

Female participation also fell from 34.7% in the preceding quarter, driven largely by rural women, whose LFPR declined from 39.2% to 37.2%.

The nature of employment differs sharply. 72% of rural women workers were self-employed, including 30.4% as contributing family workers, whereas 54.8% of urban women workers held regular wage or salaried jobs.

The figures therefore reveal differences not only in whether women participate in work, but also in the form and potential security of that employment.

Rural Employment Remains Closely Tied to Agriculture

Agriculture employed 52.9% of rural workers, with dependence particularly high among rural women: 70.7% worked in agriculture, compared with 43.9% of rural men.

Across rural and urban India combined:

  • 38.7% of workers were in agriculture;

  • 26.7% were in the secondary sector; and

  • 34.6% were in services.

These shares moved back towards their April–June 2025 levels after agriculture's employment share had risen in intervening quarters, underscoring the importance of seasonality when interpreting quarter-to-quarter movements.

How Does PLFS Measure Quarterly Employment?
The bulletin uses Current Weekly Status (CWS), based on activity during the preceding seven days. A person who worked for at least one hour is counted as employed; someone who did not work but sought or was available for work is counted as unemployed.

Policy Relevance

The quarter points to stable headline unemployment but growing pressure among young labour-market entrants and continued structural constraints on women’s employment.

  • Track participation with unemployment: Employment assessment should consider LFPR and WPR alongside the unemployment rate, particularly when participation is falling.

  • Focus on school-to-work transitions: Rising youth unemployment strengthens the case for connecting education, apprenticeships, placement services and employer demand.

  • Differentiate women’s employment: Participation targets should distinguish regular paid employment, own-account work and unpaid contributing family work.

  • Address urban female joblessness: Low participation combined with high unemployment suggests constraints involving job availability, skills, mobility, safety and care responsibilities.

  • Account for seasonality: Consecutive-quarter changes in rural employment should be interpreted alongside agricultural cycles; year-on-year comparisons provide an important additional benchmark.
